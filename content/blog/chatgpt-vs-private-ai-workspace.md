"Can't we just use ChatGPT?" is the first question in almost every firm that starts thinking about AI. It's a fair question. General assistants are remarkable. The real question is which jobs they suit, and which they don't.

## What general chatbots do brilliantly

- Drafting and rewriting: emails, newsletters, explanations of a rule change for clients.
- Brainstorming and outlining.
- Research on public information, with appropriate checking.
- Learning: "explain this standard like I'm a new hire".

For this kind of work, a general assistant is fast, flexible and inexpensive.

## Where firms hit limits

Client work is different in three ways:

1. **It depends on your data.** The answer to "which 1099s don't match a deposit?" lives in your ledger and your client folders, not on the internet. Copying files into a chat window works once; it doesn't scale, and it scatters client data.
2. **It has to be traceable.** A number in a workpaper or a date in a matter summary needs a source you can check. If an answer can't show where it came from, someone has to redo the work to verify it.
3. **It has consequences.** Emails to clients, entries in the books and filings carry your name. Tools that act without approval move that risk onto you.

## What a private AI workspace adds

- **Connections, not copies:** the firm's tools (QuickBooks, Xero, Gmail, Outlook, Drive) connect through each app's own sign-in, per person.
- **Cited answers:** every figure or date links back to the page or record it came from.
- **Agents for firm work:** tax prep, month-end close, collections and matter summaries, built around review.
- **Approval by default:** drafts wait; nothing goes to a client until a person approves it.

## A simple rule for your AI policy

> General assistants for general work. The private workspace for anything involving client records.

Write that down, name the approved tools, and train the team on it. Our [Firm AI-Use Policy Template](/resources/firm-ai-use-policy-template) includes wording you can adapt.
