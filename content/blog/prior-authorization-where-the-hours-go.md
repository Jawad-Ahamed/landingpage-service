Prior authorization is the admin task practices complain about most, and with reason. In the AMA's 2024 physician survey, practices reported completing about **39 prior authorizations per physician per week**, taking roughly **13 hours** of physician and staff time. More than nine in ten physicians said prior authorization delays care.

The clinical judgment in a prior auth takes minutes. The hours go somewhere else. Here's where.

## The workflow, step by step

1. **Recognize that a PA is needed.** Payer rules differ by plan, procedure and site of service, and they change.
2. **Gather documentation:** chart notes, imaging history, therapy notes, prior medications tried and failed.
3. **Match codes to criteria:** the CPT code for the service, the ICD-10 codes that justify it, and the payer's specific clinical criteria, such as "six weeks of conservative care".
4. **Fill the payer's form or portal,** often re-keying what's already in the chart.
5. **Find what's missing:** a referring provider NPI, a date, a signed order.
6. **Submit and follow up,** by portal, fax or phone.
7. **Handle denials and appeals,** which restart much of the above.

## Where the time really goes

Steps 2, 3 and 5 are the expensive ones. They're hunting tasks: reading through notes for a date, checking one payer's criteria against what the chart documents, and discovering a gap only after the form is half-filled. They're also the steps where nothing requires a license, only care and patience.

## What can be prepared automatically

An agent can do the preparation and leave the decisions to people:

- **Read the intake and chart notes,** with patient identifiers masked from the start.
- **Pull the relevant history:** therapy notes, imaging, medications tried.
- **Match the codes to the payer's criteria** and show which criteria are met, each with a cite to the page that proves it.
- **Flag what's missing,** for example "Referring provider NPI missing: needs review", before anyone starts the form.
- **Assemble the packet** and hold it for clinician review and sign-off.

The result is that staff time moves from hunting to checking, and clinicians review a complete packet instead of answering three rounds of questions.

## What's changing on the payer side

CMS's Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule (CMS-0057-F) requires impacted payers, including Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans, to send decisions within 72 hours for expedited requests and seven calendar days for standard ones beginning in 2026, and to support electronic prior authorization through APIs from 2027. Faster payer timelines make complete, first-time-right submissions even more valuable.
